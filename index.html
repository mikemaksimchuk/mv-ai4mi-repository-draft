Why this collection exists

The FY25 State School Aid Act directs Michigan Virtual to operate a statewide laboratory that identifies and disseminates best practices for the responsible use of artificial intelligence (AI) in schools. This library is part of how we do that.

It serves two readers at once. A Michigan educator or administrator arrives looking for something usable on Monday. A bench member needs a working bibliography that will hold up when a superintendent asks where a recommendation came from. A resource that is merely interesting fails the first reader. A resource that is merely popular fails the second.

So nothing here is listed because it circulated widely. Each resource carries a score, a tier, and a short reviewer note that says plainly what it is good for and where it falls short. Fifteen resources are marked not recommended and left in the collection on purpose — a library that shows only its winners hides its judgment.

What changed in FY27

As of June 2026 the five standing subcommittees became benches: pools of educators we draw on for specific initiatives. The Workgroup defines a piece of work, passes it to bench co-chairs, and an ad-hoc team carries it out. This collection is the shared starting point those teams inherit, so that a team convened this spring and a team convened next fall are working from the same shelf.

How we scored these resources

Two layers. Five shared criteria every bench applies the same way, worth 60 points. Two specialized criteria that change by bench, worth 40. We score each criterion from 0 to 4.

The shared rubric — 60 points

B1

Source credibility and authority

Does the publisher's standing justify a district acting on this? Government agencies, peer-reviewed research, university presses and established associations score high. Anonymous or search-optimized content scores low. A named practitioner with a track record can score well; an unnamed one cannot.

× 3
B2

Accuracy and currency

Factually sound, and current enough to act on. A 2023 tool recommendation is usually wrong now. Learning science ages more slowly and we judge it accordingly. Work that predates generative AI can still score well on concept, but we never present it as current practice.
Scoring 0 or 1 here caps the resource at conditional

× 3
B3

Practitioner usability

Can a Michigan educator actually do something with this? Templates, checklists, sample language and decision tools score high. Material that describes a problem without offering a path through it scores lower, however well written. A two-page protocol a team will read beats a 200-page report they will not.

× 3
B4

Equity, safety and sustainability

The charter's four-part test: equitable, safe, sustainable, lasting. Access gaps, bias, learner variability and multilingual students. Student privacy and age-appropriate protection. Whether a district can still afford it in year three, and what it costs the environment. Whether it survives the next model release. Silence on any of the four is a finding.

× 3
B5

Independence and transparency

Is there a sales motive, and is it disclosed? We do not exclude vendor material and some of it is excellent — but a buyer's guide written by a seller in that category is marketing. Three questions: is funding disclosed, would the advice change if the publisher sold nothing, and is it gated behind lead capture.
Scoring 0 or 1 here caps the resource at conditional

× 3

Two criteria can override the total. A resource scoring 0 or 1 on accuracy or independence is capped at conditional no matter how well it scores elsewhere. Without that gate, high usability scores let polished commercial material climb into the recommended tier — and a workgroup that includes the Michigan Department of Education, the Michigan Education Association and AFT Michigan cannot publish marketing as neutral guidance.

Using the explorer

Filters stack. Every filter shows how many resources it would return, so you can see a dead end before you reach it.

Step 1

Start with your bench

Or ignore benches entirely. Many resources serve several, and the "also serves" line on each record tells you which.

Step 2

Narrow by what you need

Filter by topic, format, audience, cost, or whether the publisher sells something. Search matches titles, publishers, tags and reviewer notes.

Step 3

Read the score profile

The seven bars under each score show the criterion-by-criterion profile. Bar height carries the meaning, so it stays readable without relying on color.

Step 4

Open the full record

Select any resource for the reviewer note, the full scoring breakdown, link verification status, and the route to the source.

On links: we confirmed 158 URLs directly. The rest are publisher or organization pages marked verify before publishing — they almost certainly resolve, but nobody clicked them one by one. Three records are flagged further still, where we found only a secondary source. Check those before anything goes on ai4mi.org.

Ask the collection

Describe what you are working on in plain language. The assistant searches all 242 records, reads the ones that match, and answers with the scores and caveats attached, so you can open any record it names.
